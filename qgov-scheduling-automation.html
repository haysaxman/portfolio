QGov Scheduling Automation

Context

I worked on the design of a device scheduling system intended to replace an Excel-based workflow. As part of a competitive tender, we were given a fixed set of requirements to adhere to while also addressing clear scalability challenges. The existing workflow limited how much work could be scheduled and managed, and most of the operational effort was spent manually selecting sites and validating schedules. The complexity was increased by different device types, each with their own processes and constraints.

Note: Any specifics of this project have been removed due to contractual obligation.

Design Challenge

The core challenge was to transform a highly manual, spreadsheet-driven workflow into a scalable, automated system without losing the flexibility that users relied on in Excel.

The solution needed to enable individuals to manage more work, support multiple device types and providers, and provide better visibility across schedules, locations, and related equipment for both the government officers and providers within different roles.

Core challenges:

  • Map-based scheduling for different devices
  • Providing and comparing estimates to vendor invoices
  • Robust information architecture
  • Scalability

Approach

I focused on designing an automated scheduling workflow in Figma that reduced manual decision-making while still allowing human oversight. This included enabling vendors to log progress and confirm completed work, as well as streamlining how they reviewed and provided feedback on proposed schedules.

I also helped with the business analysis with the government project team to unpack the requirements and understand the core need for the device scheduling, then worked closely with the developers to create a plan for the scheduling algorithm.

A key part of the design was introducing a more robust data model that clearly associated devices, locations, vendors, and schedules, and allowed users to easily navigate between related information. I held frequent design review sessions with the client’s team, these regular check-ins helped maintain transparency and build a shared understanding of the evolving product, particularly as early versions of the application lacked visual polish.

Figma components used in the tender.

Outcomes & Learnings

A key insight was uncovering incorrect early assumptions: scheduling was initially designed during the tender around device movement from a home base to site, but the primary use case involved devices moving from site to site. This meant that changes to one location had cascading effects on subsequent schedules, reinforcing the need for more interconnected, system-aware design thinking earlier in the process.

In retrospect, more discovery-focused activities such as early prototyping and user testing would have surfaced differences in assumptions and understanding sooner and reduced ambiguity.

The project ceased to continue out of convenience for the government department, which is unfortunate as I enjoyed the time collaborating with the government project team, to solving complex issues and drive better user-experience within government workflows.
